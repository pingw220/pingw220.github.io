## Motivation

Automatic music transcription (AMT) outputs sheet music, and sheet music serves two purposes:
musicians **read** it, and it can be **played back**. A transcription can look like a clean,
faithful score yet sound unlike the performance, or sound right yet be painful to read. A single
metric cannot capture both.

## Approach

We evaluate transcriptions along two complementary dimensions:

- **Notation similarity** to a reference score, using metrics from the optical music recognition
  literature.
- **Playback similarity** to the original performance. We compared a wide range of candidate
  methods against a listening study with over 100 participants and 230 piano recordings, covering
  23 works, 30 performers, and six composers.

## Findings

- The playback metric that agrees best with human judgments, **CLEWS**, is also the cheapest to run.
- Across **24 pipelines** (eight audio-to-MIDI models × three MIDI-to-score converters), the two
  dimensions favor different systems. The MIDI-to-score converter largely determines which
  objective a pipeline does well on.
- **Rubato**, a new end-to-end system, substantially improves notation similarity while staying
  competitive on playback similarity.
